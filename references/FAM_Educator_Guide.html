Section 1

About the game

When Mira Calls is a narrative adventure game for ages 11–13. A child receives a letter from an aunt they never knew they had. She is missing and needs their help. What follows is a journey across three worlds - a fishing village, a busy town square, and a hilltop observatory - each in quiet crisis. At every stop the child figures out what went wrong and why. The cybersecurity lessons arrive through the story, not as interruptions to it.

🎮

Format

Browser-based adventure game. No download, no account, no personal data collected. Progress saves locally on the device. Works on tablets, laptops, and desktop computers.

⏱

Time

Each world takes 45–90 minutes. Full game is 3–5 hours. Individual worlds work as standalone sessions. See Section 3 for detailed timing.

👥

Group size

Individual, pairs, small groups of 3–4, or whole class on a shared screen. Each model has different advantages - see Section 2.

Topics covered

World 0 - Home: Tutorial only. No lesson content.

World 1 - Seabright: Phishing, red flags, scam ads, responding to scams.

Lighthouse Bridge: Password strength, passphrases, the strongbox mechanic.

World 2 - Sunshare Square: Digital footprint, internet permanence, online trust and grooming warning signs, cyberbullying (all three positions), relational sharing, AI nonconsensual imagery (signpost), password hygiene and reuse.

World 3 - Observatory: Smishing, vishing, MFA, social engineering, the final synthesis test.

Sensitive content: Cyberbullying including the perpetrator position; online grooming warning signs; AI-generated nonconsensual imagery (brief signpost only). See Section 6 for safeguarding guidance.

Not covered in depth: Radicalisation and extremism (named as a pattern only); financial fraud beyond email and ad scams; explicit sexual content of any kind.

Section 2

How to use it

The game is built on one principle that matters for facilitation: lessons land best when they arrive through experience, not instruction. The game delivers lessons through character stories and choices. Your role is to create space for reflection after play - not to pre-teach the content before it.

Three models of use

Individual play

Each child plays on their own device at their own pace. Follow with structured discussion or the reflection tool (Section 7). Works as homework between sessions if devices are available.

Paired or small group

Two to four children share a device and discuss choices together as they play. The choice points become group decisions. Disagreements are productive. Best model for deepening understanding.

Whole class, shared screen

One device projected to the class. Pause at each decision point for group discussion before continuing. Time-intensive but high-impact. Best for introducing the game and ensuring all students encounter key moments together.

The most important facilitation principle - bookend, do not interrupt. Resist pausing mid-scene to deliver the lesson. The scene is the lesson. Pausing breaks the emotional momentum that makes it stick. Let students play through a complete scene or world, then discuss. Every question in Section 5 is designed to be asked after play, not during it.

Five-minute activator - run before World 1

The stranger question: Ask students to think of three things they would not tell a stranger who walked up to them on the street. Name them. Then ask: are any of those things currently visible on your public social media profile to anyone who searches for you?

Takes five minutes, no devices needed. Creates the cognitive frame that makes World 2 land. Do not extend - leave it open and let the game close it.

Section 3

Timing and session planning

15–20 min

World 0 - Home (Tutorial)

Movement, inventory, inspecting objects, choices, saving. No lesson content. Can be skipped for confident players. Do not skip for less confident players - the tutorial builds the confidence that makes the rest accessible.

50–70 min

World 1 - Seabright

6 scenes + 2 minigames + 6 badges. Complete arc, satisfying resolution. Recommended as the first full session. Minigame 1 (Real or Fake?): 8–12 min. Minigame 2 (Danger on the Screen): 6–10 min.

25–35 min

The Lighthouse Bridge

4 scenes + 1 passphrase minigame. Usually played as the end of the Seabright session or the start of Sunshare Square. The passphrase set here carries through to the Observatory. Remind students to remember their passphrase.

70–90 min

World 2 - Sunshare Square

9 scenes + 2 minigames + 4 badges. The longest world, the most emotionally complex material. Allow extra discussion time after this world. Consider splitting across two sessions: Scenes 1–4 first, then Minigame 2 and Scenes 4.5–5.

55–75 min

World 3 - The Observatory

7 scenes + 1 final minigame + 4 badges. The final minigame (Who Is the Real Aunt Mira?) requires synthesis across all worlds - allow 10–15 minutes. Do not split across sessions - the emotional payoff of the reunion requires the twin reveal to be fresh.

Total: 3–5 hours of gameplay. A four-session programme - one per world, combining Lighthouse with Seabright - works well within a typical school half-term unit. Each world can also stand alone.

World 1 - Seabright

Discussion guide · Section 5a

A fishing village paralysed by scam messages. Emails pretending to be from the Harbour Authority, fake delivery texts, an official-sounding phone call, fake ads on the village tablet. Accounts drained, boats grounded. The child works through what happened with each fisherman. Key emotional moment: Bram waited three days to tell anyone because he was embarrassed. Old Finn says: "We are not stupid people."
Phishing SpotterRed Flag DetectiveMessage DetectiveCountdown BusterFirst ResponderQuick Reactions

After playing - discussion questions

1

"Old Finn said they are not stupid people - but they still fell for it. Why do smart people get caught by scams?"

Key insight: scams are designed by professionals to trigger urgency and fear, which bypass careful thinking. This is not a character flaw. Paired discussion first, then share with the group.

2

"Bram waited three days before telling anyone. What did the delay cost him - and why do you think he waited?"

Opens the conversation about shame as a barrier to reporting. Key principle: telling someone quickly is always better than waiting, even if waiting feels safer.

3

"What are the four steps to take if you think you have been scammed - and why does the order matter?"

Tell someone → do not engage → report → block. The order matters because telling a trusted adult first means an adult can help with the next steps. Ask for the order before supplying it.

4

"If a message arrived on your phone right now saying your account would be closed in two hours - walk me through exactly what you would do."

Brings it into their real life. Expected: pause, check the sender address independently, do not click the link, verify through the real website or app. If they say "I would just check it" - ask how, specifically.

Sensitivity note

Some students may have personal experience of a family member being scammed. The shame framing in Bram's story may resonate more than expected. If a student discloses that financial harm has occurred to a child or family, follow your school's safeguarding protocol.

The Lighthouse Bridge

Discussion guide · Section 5b

At the top of the cliff the child knocks on the lighthouse door. A woman opens it - she has Aunt Mira's face. She is not Mira. She is Celia. She gives the child the Golden Knot, Mira's Notebook, and the Strongbox. The strongbox needs a passphrase. Key metaphor: "A knot holds not by its complexity, but by its length and its commitment to itself."
Password strength understoodPassphrase set and memorisedShadow Man introduced

After playing - discussion questions

1

"Why is 'tiger cloud lamp river' a stronger password than 'Pa$$w0rd1'? Explain it to someone who has never heard of passphrases."

Ask them to explain it peer-to-peer, not to you. The answer: 23 characters vs 9, length multiplies difficulty enormously, no predictable pattern. If they struggle, ask: which is longer?

2

"Celia said the Shadow Man follows gaps - weak passwords, unlocked boxes, things left unprotected. What gaps might exist in your own digital life right now?"

Expect answers about reused passwords, easy-to-guess passwords, accounts not updated. Do not shame specific answers - the point is awareness, not confession.

Follow-up activity

Passphrase challenge: Give students two minutes to create a passphrase of four or more random, unrelated words - nothing connected to their name, pets, school, or interests. Then ask: how would you remember it? This surfaces the practical challenge and opens the password manager conversation without requiring a full lesson.

World 2 - Sunshare Square

Discussion guide · Section 5c

Sunshare Square is built around sharing - everything open, everything visible. But someone has been using that openness against young people the same age as the player. Jaylen's gaming account taken over via security questions answered from his public posts. Priya impersonated using her profile information. Soren asked to keep an online friendship secret and move to an unknown app. Maya silent in a group chat where harm is happening. And Amara, quietly on a bench - AI-generated fake images of her, shared around her school. This is the most emotionally dense world. Build in discussion time after it.
Footprint TrackerDeliberate SharerPassword HygienistSafe Editor

After playing - discussion questions

1

"What could a stranger learn about Priya from her public profile - without her realising she had shared any of it?"

School name, teacher's name, weekly routine, friend's username, rough home location. Key insight: it is the combination, not any single post. Then: is there anything like that on yours?

2

"Soren was asked to keep an online friendship secret and move to an unknown app. Why is the secrecy request the red flag - even before anything else happened?"

Genuine relationships do not require hiding from the people who care about you. The request to be secret is itself the signal. Ask: what would you do? Who would you tell?

3

"Maya had been in the group for three days without saying anything. The game said that was a choice. Do you agree - and what were her options?"

The bystander position. Silence is read as approval by the person being targeted. Expect disagreement - some students will argue Maya was protecting herself. That is a productive disagreement to have.

4

"Jaylen admitted he had laughed at content in a group chat last year. He did not see himself as a bully. What does that tell us about how online harm works?"

Most harm is not done by people who identify as bullies - it is done by people who did not think about the person on the other end. Give this question space. Do not rush to the moral.

Sensitivity note - three areas requiring extra care in this world

Cyberbullying (Scene 4.5): Students may identify strongly with one of the three positions. Allow space without putting individuals on the spot. If a student discloses current bullying, follow your school's anti-bullying and safeguarding protocols.

Online grooming warning signs (Scene 3.5): The secrecy and platform migration scenario may prompt a student to disclose a concerning online contact. Treat as a potential safeguarding matter - thank the student, do not probe further, involve the DSL immediately.

AI-generated nonconsensual imagery (Scene 5): Introduced briefly through Amara. If a student discloses that this has happened to them or someone they know, this is a serious safeguarding matter. Do not manage it in the classroom - involve the DSL immediately.

World 3 - The Observatory

Discussion guide · Section 5d

Two identical women wait at the top of the hill. Both have Aunt Mira's face. Both say the same thing. One is real. One is a trap. The child explores the Observatory - learning smishing, vishing, and MFA through what they find - while watching both women interact differently with each discovery. The impostor creates urgency, claims authority, tries to bypass careful thinking. The real Mira waits. The final minigame asks the child to use everything learned to identify which one is real. The answer is in the behaviour, not the face.
Smishing SpotterVishing SpotterSecond Lock (MFA)Social Engineer Spotter

After playing - discussion questions

1

"How did you work out which one was really Aunt Mira? What were the specific signals that told you the other one was the impostor?"

This is the synthesis question - it contains the whole game. Expected: urgency ("we are running out of time"), authority ("you have to trust me"), pressure to skip thinking, knowledge she should not have had. Do not supply the answer.

2

"The game called it social engineering. What does that mean - and where else might you see those same tactics used?"

Manipulating feelings rather than breaking systems. Ask where else the pattern appears - in advertising, peer pressure, group dynamics, online recruitment. It extends far beyond scams.

3

"Why does two-factor authentication make an account so much harder to access - even if someone knows your password?"

Something you know AND something you have. Practical follow-up: which of your accounts have two-factor on? How would you turn it on for your email?

4

"Mira said: 'He doesn't stop existing - but he stops mattering to the people who know what you know now.' What did she mean?"

The risks do not disappear. Knowledge changes the relationship to them. Allow it to land quietly - do not rush to a follow-up question.

Section 6

Follow-up activities

Four offline activities that extend the learning without a screen. Each works as a standalone lesson, homework task, or end-of-unit activity.

Activity 1

The Profile Audit

20–30 minPairs or small groupsConnects to: Sunshare Square

Create a fictional social media profile for a made-up 12-year-old. Give them a name, a school, a few posts, a profile photo description. Swap with another group and answer: what does a stranger now know? What could they use, and how? The fictional character protects students from feeling personally exposed while making the lesson concrete.

Activity 2

Spot the Red Flag

15–20 minIndividual or pairsConnects to: Seabright, Observatory

Give students five written messages - a mix of real and fake. Identify the red flags in each fake one. Then ask: which was hardest to spot, and why? The hardest ones are always the most personalised - professional language, real-sounding details, no obvious errors. Lesson: sophistication is not legitimacy.

Activity 3

Three Positions - One Scenario

25–35 minGroups of 3Connects to: Sunshare Square Scene 4.5

Give each group a written cyberbullying scenario. Each person takes one position - targeted, bystander, contributor. Each writes three sentences: what they are feeling, what they are thinking, what they could do differently. Groups then share across positions. The perpetrator position is the most important to give space to - it is the one most likely to change actual behaviour.

Activity 4

A Letter to a Younger Sibling

20–30 minIndividualConnects to: all worlds

Write a short letter to a fictional 9-year-old sibling about to get their first phone. Explain one thing learned from the game - in language a 9-year-old would understand. No jargon. No lists. Just one thing, explained clearly. If they can explain it simply, they understand it deeply. This is the best assessment of understanding in this guide.

🛡

Safeguarding and disclosure protocol

Section 8 · For all facilitators

When Mira Calls addresses topics that may prompt a student to disclose something that has happened to them or someone they know. This is not a risk - it is often a positive outcome of the game creating a safe context to talk. What matters is how the disclosure is handled.

Potential disclosure triggers by world

Seabright

Financial scam affecting the student or family. Action: acknowledge, do not probe, refer to DSL if financial harm to a child or family is involved.

Sunshare Sq.

Current cyberbullying targeting (any position). Concerning online contact involving secrecy or platform migration. AI-generated harmful imagery - this is a serious safeguarding matter. Action: treat any disclosure from this world as a potential safeguarding matter; involve the DSL promptly.

Observatory

Recognition of social engineering patterns in an existing real relationship or online contact. Action: if a student identifies a real relationship matching the pattern, do not dismiss. Take details and involve the DSL.

If a student discloses during a session

1

Stay calm and listen

Do not show alarm. Say: "Thank you for telling me. You did the right thing." Do not ask probing questions or seek more detail than the student volunteers.

2

Do not promise confidentiality

You may need to share what the student has told you with the designated safeguarding lead. Be honest: "I will need to share this with someone who can help, but I will do that carefully."

3

Do not investigate

Your role is to receive, record, and refer - not to investigate. Ask only: "Can you tell me a little more about what happened?" Do not press for detail.

4

Record and refer promptly

Write down what was said as close to verbatim as possible using the student's own words. Include time and date. Refer to your Designated Safeguarding Lead the same day.

Important: This guide does not replace your institution's safeguarding policy. All disclosures must be handled in accordance with your school or organisation's procedures and the relevant national framework. If you are unsure, contact your DSL immediately rather than waiting.

"He doesn't stop existing. But he stops mattering - to the people who know what you know now."

That is what Aunt Mira says at the end of the journey. The risks of the online world do not disappear when the game ends. But a child who can name the tactics - urgency, fear, authority, secrecy, manufactured belonging - is fundamentally more resilient than one who cannot.

Your role is not to be the expert. The game is the expert. Your role is to create the space in which what the child learned can become something they own - a habit of thought, a reflex of caution, a confidence that they can figure it out. The discussions in this guide deepen that ownership. The activities practise it. The reflection tool evidences it.

If a student leaves your sessions with one thing, let it be this: when something feels wrong online, telling someone is always the right call. Not because the adult will fix it. Because the adult can help them decide what to do next - and they do not have to carry it alone.
